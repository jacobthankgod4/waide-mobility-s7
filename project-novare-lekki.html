Indoor spatial mapping and digital twin development, Novare Lekki Mall.

A three-level retail environment in Lekki, Lagos, digitised into a structured indoor spatial model with separate pedestrian and vehicle networks, shelf-level indexing and step-free routing.

01 · Project overview

A mall mapped as a system, not a picture.

The project turns the mall's floor plans and field observations into a routable indoor dataset: units, corridors, level connectors, facilities, parking and retail shelves, each with attributes that a navigation interface can query.

The work sits between surveying and software. Plans were georeferenced and digitised, a feature schema was defined, pedestrian and vehicle networks were built separately, and the result was tested by walking the routes on site.

Measurement basis: the 22,000 m² figure is the reported mall area covered by the mapping scope. Store, bay and shelf counts are counts of digitised features, not sales or footfall metrics.

02 · Location

Lekki, Lagos, Nigeria.

Novare Lekki Mall is a multi-level retail development with an anchor supermarket, 150+ store footprints and a large car park that feeds visitors into the building.

The venue's scale is the reason the project exists. A visitor arriving by car has to find a bay, find a entrance, cross a level change and then locate a unit or a shelf. That is four separate spatial problems in one trip.

03 · Context

Why indoor mapping is harder than it looks.

Outdoor mapping has a mature data model and a global community of contributors. Indoor mapping has neither. Every venue defines its own levels, units, access rules and naming conventions.

A mall adds retail churn: tenants change, fit-outs move walls, promotions block concourses, and a route that was correct last quarter may now be wrong. The dataset has to be maintainable, not just accurate on the day it was built.

  • Floor plans are often PDFs or CAD files with no coordinate reference.
  • Level naming is inconsistent between plans, signage and tenant lists.
  • Service corridors and back-of-house areas are not on public plans.
  • Parking geometry has to be captured separately from the retail floors.
  • Accessible routes depend on lift status and temporary obstructions.

04 · Technical challenge

One model, two networks, three levels.

The central challenge was to represent a car trip and a walking trip in the same spatial model without conflating them. A vehicle route ends where a pedestrian route begins, and the handover point has to be explicit.

A second challenge was depth of search. Finding a store is useful; finding a product inside a 900-shelf supermarket requires a shelf-level index, which means the anchor tenant is mapped at a finer granularity than the rest of the mall.

Network separation

Vehicle and pedestrian graphs are distinct, with defined transfer nodes at parking lifts and entrances.

Level connectors

Lifts, stairs, escalators and ramps are modelled as edges with cost and access attributes.

08 · Tools and technologies

Open tools, chosen for control.

  • JOSM for indoor digitisation, tagging and geometry editing.
  • OpenStreetMap-style indoor tagging for levels, units, corridors, doors and facilities.
  • Georeferencing against available imagery and control points.
  • Graph construction for separate pedestrian and vehicle networks.
  • Dijkstra shortest-path routing with mode-specific edge costs.
  • Browser-based delivery for visitor access without an app install.

No proprietary computer vision or generative model is credited with the digitised geometry. The parking bays, units and routes were drawn and checked by hand.

09 · Mapping and digitisation workflow

Draw, tag, connect, check.

A floor plan gives the outline. The work is in the topology: which corridor connects to which lift, which door is public, which edge is step-free.

  1. Trace the building shell and floor boundary.
  2. Draw units and assign tenant and category attributes.
  3. Draw circulation space and connect it to entrances and lifts.
  4. Add facilities and accessible points of interest.
  5. Build the pedestrian graph and a separate step-free graph.
  6. Build the vehicle graph across Parking Level A.
  7. Index shelves inside the anchor supermarket.
  8. Verify on site and correct the topology.

Quality rules applied

  • No orphaned corridors or unreachable units.
  • Every level change uses a named connector.
  • Vehicle and pedestrian edges never share a node without a transfer point.
  • Every store has a stable identifier.
  • Step-free edges are tagged and validated, not inferred.

10 · Vehicle and pedestrian routing

Two graphs, one journey.

The visitor's journey begins in a car and continues on foot. The model handles that by keeping the networks separate and defining where they meet.

Vehicle routing uses the parking layout: aisles, bay access and exit points. Pedestrian routing uses the retail floor: corridors, doors, lifts, escalators and stairs. A transfer node connects a parking bay to the nearest pedestrian entrance or lift lobby.

Dijkstra's algorithm computes the shortest path over the relevant graph. Edge costs differ by mode: a vehicle edge is not weighted like a walking edge, and a step-free route may accept a longer distance to avoid a stair.

13 · Validation and quality control

Checked against the building.

  • Geometry reviewed for gaps, overlaps and unreachable areas.
  • Every level connector checked against the physical lift or stair.
  • Routes walked end to end, including at least one step-free alternative.
  • Entrances and closures confirmed with the venue team.
  • Vehicle routes checked against the parking layout and one-way flows.
  • Findings logged and corrected before the route layer was accepted.

This is project-level quality control. It is not a formal certification, an independent audit or a compliance statement.
