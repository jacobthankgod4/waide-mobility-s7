Retail

Malls and supermarkets

A shopper who cannot find an item leaves. The sale never reaches the shop and the report never explains why. That is the gap a map closes first.

What visitors need

  • Search by product, brand, category or service
  • A route to the aisle and shelf, not just the store
  • Parking, lifts and toilets on the same map
  • Step-free paths for wheelchairs and pushchairs

What operators get

  • Top searches with no matching destination
  • Drop-off zones where routes are abandoned
  • Zone traffic and corridor flow, weekly
  • Traffic past a unit for leasing conversations

In a pilot we measure search completion, drop-off zone, top unmet searches, QR scan to route, zone dwell change and route completion time. Figures are published with a baseline and sample size.

Guides: mall navigation · parking garages · Novare Lekki pilot

Healthcare

Hospitals and clinics

A patient arriving for an appointment should not have to ask three people where to go. Wayfinding is part of the care experience, and it takes pressure off the front desk.

What visitors need

  • Department, clinic, ward and diagnostic room search
  • Step-free routes from the car park or bus stop
  • Pharmacy, billing, toilets and exits on the map
  • Clear routes in local languages

What operators get

  • Which departments visitors cannot find
  • Where people stop and turn back
  • Demand for services by zone and time
  • A record of route completion, not a lobby guess

Guides: hospital navigation · libraries · museums

Transport

Airports and stations

Transport buildings punish anyone who does not already know them. The map has to work across levels, between buildings and under time pressure.

What visitors need

  • Gate, platform, check-in and baggage search
  • Lifts, escalators and step-free routes
  • Lounges, shops, prayer rooms and toilets
  • Directions that survive a level change

What operators get

  • What passengers search for and fail to find
  • Concourse and corridor movement by zone
  • Where signage is doing the work, and where it is not
  • A map that updates when a gate or route changes

Guides: airports · train stations · subway systems

Education

Universities and campuses

First-year students, visiting parents and conference guests all arrive without the mental map that staff have. A campus map shortens the first week.

What visitors need

  • Building, lecture hall, lab and office search
  • Library, cafeteria and student services on the map
  • Step-free routes between buildings
  • Directions that work without a campus app

What operators get

  • Which buildings and services visitors search for
  • Where new students get stuck in the first weeks
  • Movement between buildings and across campuses
  • Evidence for signage and wayfinding spend

Guides: universities · libraries

Workplace

Offices and corporate estates

A visitor arriving for a meeting should reach reception without calling for help. Staff should find a meeting room, a desk or a service without asking around.

What visitors need

  • Reception, meeting rooms and tenant directory
  • Lifts, stairs and step-free routes
  • Cafeteria, toilets and parking on the map
  • Directions in the phone browser

What operators get

  • Which tenants and rooms visitors cannot find
  • Reception load by hour and by entrance
  • Movement between buildings on an estate
  • A directory that updates with tenancy changes

Guides: office buildings · smart building integration

Industrial

Warehouses and logistics sites

Large industrial sites lose time to search: a contractor looking for a bay, a new starter looking for a zone, a driver looking for the right gate.

What people need

  • Bay, zone, gate and facility search
  • Routes across large floors and yards
  • Safety points, muster areas and exits on the map
  • Access rules for staff, contractors and visitors

What operators get

  • Which locations people search for most
  • Where new staff and contractors get lost
  • Time lost to internal wayfinding, by zone
  • A map that changes with racking and layout

Guides: warehouses · manufacturing · data centres

Venues and events

Stadiums, arenas and cultural venues

On event day, thousands of people arrive at once and none of them know the building. Directions have to be clear at the gate, on the concourse and at the seat.

What visitors need

  • Gate, block, seat, stand and hall search
  • Concessions, toilets and first aid on the map
  • Step-free routes and accessible seating
  • Exits and transport links after the event

What operators get

  • Which gates and facilities people look for
  • Concourse movement by zone and by hour
  • Where queues and bottlenecks form
  • A map that changes with each event layout

Guides: sports venues · convention centres · theme parks · casinos · cruise ships · hotels

Questions

Common questions

Which venue types are live today?

One: Novare Lekki Mall in Lagos, a six-month pilot covering 22,000 m². The other venue types on this page use the same platform, but they are not yet deployed as references.

Can the map work across several buildings?

Yes. A campus or estate can be mapped as one experience with several buildings, as long as the routes between them are in scope.

Do we need to change our signage?

No. The map is a second route, not a replacement. Most venues add QR points at the entrances they already have, and keep their existing signs.

Can it be branded for our venue?

Yes. The visitor experience carries the venue name and colours, and the operator dashboard is separate.

What does a pilot cost for one venue?

A pilot is a fixed fee quoted after a floor-plan review. Platform fees for a full deployment start at US$399 a month for a small venue; see pricing.

Tell us which building.

Send a floor plan and a floor count. We will come back with the scope, the price and what a pilot would measure in the first 90 days.
